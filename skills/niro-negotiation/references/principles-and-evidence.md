## Negotiation as a science
- **[N]** "Negotiation is a learned skill grounded in science, more than art." About 80% of success happens in preparation, not in the room. It can be learned by anyone, and you don't need a dramatic persona "like FBI hostage negotiators". (Nibble Technology webinar on AI and the future of negotiation, Oct 2024.)
- **[N]** AI can reach Pareto-optimal outcomes more consistently than humans because it removes the personal biases and pride that block win-win deals. One-sided outcomes win the short term but damage long-term relationships, because unhappy counterparts don't come back. (Nibble, 2024.)

## First offers and anchoring
- **[N]** Always aim to go first, to anchor the conversation around your preferred outcome. The mechanism is insufficient adjustment from an anchor (Kahneman): when you go first, the final agreement is more likely to land in your favour. (Nibble; Citrus Studios write-up of a Niro entrepreneur session.)
- **[N]** "An anchor without a rationale is just a random number." The rationale makes the offer sound thought-out and respectful, so it's more likely to be taken seriously. (Citrus Studios.)
- Don't confuse this with the LBS article *7 negotiation tactics you don't see in the movies* (2018). That one is by **Thomas Mussweiler**, not Niro. Its finding that "the least skilled negotiator who makes the first offer beats the most skilled one who doesn't" is Mussweiler's.

## Concessions
- **[N]** Concessions should be "exponentially decreasing", showing you're nearing your limit without giving away too much too soon. Every time you concede, ask for something in return. (Citrus Studios.)
- **[LBS]** From Niro's closing slide: "Make concessions slowly. Ask for concessions in aggregate."

## Multi-issue deals and MESOs
- **[N]** "It is short-sighted to focus simply on salary." "Haggling over a price like this can quickly diminish an integrative negotiation to a contentious one." (LBS Think, *How to negotiate your next job offer*, Mar 2019.)
- **[N]** Negotiating one issue after another backfires: "Once you've agreed a salary you might then move onto other aspects of the role, like holiday and location, which can further build resentment."
- **[N]** MESOs: "You need to package elements of your negotiation position into choices that take into account all factors that make up your offer. This puts the conversation on a collaborative path, while ensuring you achieve outcomes on issues that matter to you." "MESOs are powerful ways to methodically express what is really important to you without looking too assertive."
  - Method: (1) build a scoring system that compares unlike issues; (2) weight each issue against the others; (3) build three packages of equal total value; (4) present all three at once. His example issues: salary, location, holiday, medical cover, executive training, childcare, flexible working, which group or department.

## Crisis, conflict and losses
From **[N]** LBS Think, *Negotiating losses in times of crisis* (Stefan Stern & Niro Sivanathan, Mar 2020):
- **Loss aversion.** "Losses loom larger." The pain of a loss is about twice the pleasure of an equal gain, and fear of loss makes disputes worse.
- **Three approaches.** Power-based (coercion, threats, resources), rights-based (contracts, legal standards, fairness) and interests-based (underlying needs, values, desires). Prefer interests. Fairness is subjective and nebulous.
- **The orange.** Two sisters split an orange in half. One wanted the peel, the other the juice. Unpacking interests would have given both of them everything.
- **Reframe.** Move from looking back ("we agreed...") to looking forward ("how do we both benefit?").
- **BATNA** determines negotiating power. A stronger alternative gives more leverage, whatever the difference in resources.
- **Medium.** Prefer richer channels (audio-visual over audio-only over text) and real-time conversation. Don't restrict yourself to email.
- **Threats.** Only make threats you'll carry out, or you lose credibility. Use them to push toward interests, not to punish. Ignore threats aimed at you and don't retaliate.
- **Biases.** The fundamental attribution error: "We judge other people by their actions and ourselves by our intentions." Egocentric bias leads to unfounded overconfidence.
- **Emotions.** Control your own behaviour first, and others' follows. Treat the negotiation as joint problem-solving, not a tug-of-war.
- Recommended reading: *Getting to Yes* (Fisher & Ury); *Negotiation Genius* (Malhotra & Bazerman). Niro also recorded a bonus Q&A on *Getting to Yes* for the *Something You Should Know* podcast.

## Persuasion: the dilution effect
- **[N]** TED 2021, *The counterintuitive way to be more persuasive* (TEDx LBS 2019 version: *What if your arguments don't add up?*). People average the strength of the information they hear rather than adding it up, so weak or irrelevant points dilute strong ones.
  - **Dinnerware:** a 24-piece set was valued higher than a 40-piece set containing the same 24 pieces plus some broken cups and saucers (TED version: about £390 vs £192; LBS slide: £141 vs £95).
  - **Tim vs Tom:** both study 31 hours a week. Adding irrelevant details about Tom (siblings, plays pool) lowered his predicted grades.
  - **Drug ads:** listing minor side effects alongside major ones made a drug seem *less* risky (published in *Nature Human Behaviour*).
- **[N]** "Stick to your two or three strong arguments and drop all the others. I see it all the time in M&A transactions or negotiations or pitches to angel investors. They pepper their slides with '15 ways we're going to disrupt the fintech industry.'" "All of your arguments may sound good to you... step back, maybe run them by a colleague and really think about which are your strongest." "Quality trumps quantity." (Queen's Smith Business Insight.)

## Influence toolkit (negotiation-relevant slice) [LBS]
From Niro's "Unlocking Your Power of Influence" sessions, May 2026. The full version is in `ypo-lbs-2026/references/professors/niro-sivanathan-influence.md`.
- **Power vs influence.** Formal power (coercion, reward) gets compliance. Influence gets commitment. Ethics matter: use these tools for outcomes both sides will be glad of.
- **Three families of influence:** procedural (agenda, sequencing, who speaks when), cognitive (biases) and interpersonal (Cialdini's six).
- **Framing.** Same beer: £11.32 when bought from a resort hotel, £7.49 from a rundown grocery store. The context sets the price.
- **Rotten apple / decoy.** Adding a strictly worse option (Print Only at the same price as Print + Web) moved Print + Web from 32% to 84%.
- **Extreme aversion.** The same £1,200 camera was chosen by 57% when it was the middle option and 24% when it was the cheapest. Surround the option you prefer with two others.
- **Certainty effect.** People overpay to get to 0% or 100%. Communicate certainty.
- **Representativeness.** "3 out of 4" beats "75 out of 100".
- **Confirmation bias (the Wason card task).** Only about 10% look for disconfirming evidence. Test your own read of the deal by looking for what would prove it wrong.
- **Keep vs cancel.** How you word the question changes which features people weigh.
- **Closing-slide rules:** a negative frame encourages risk-seeking and a positive frame risk-aversion; make concessions slowly; ask for concessions as a package; surround the option you prefer with extremes.

## Posture [LBS]
- **Scout vs Soldier.** Update your map; don't defend it. Diagnostic questions: *Where is my map wrong? What am I defending that I should be questioning? What gorilla am I not seeing?*
- **Pilot-induced oscillation.** Gripping the controls harder under turbulence makes things worse. In negotiation terms: don't over-correct after one bad exchange.

## Research [R]
- **Sivanathan & Galinsky, *Power and Overconfidence*** (SSRN), and **Fast, Sivanathan, Mayer & Galinsky (2012), *Power and overconfident decision-making*.** Feeling powerful leads to overconfident decisions, with financial losses and knock-on effects in negotiations.
- **Sivanathan, Molden, Galinsky & Ku (2008), *The promise and peril of self-affirmation in de-escalation of commitment*.** Affirming your self-worth can help you let go of a failing course of action.
- **Gunia, Sivanathan & Galinsky (2009), *Vicarious entrapment: Your sunk costs, my escalation of commitment*.** Seeing someone else's sunk costs, especially someone you feel close to, can pull you into escalating too.
- **HBR, with Freek Vermeulen, on escalation of commitment ("doubling down")**, and **HBR 2017, *Why we prefer dominant leaders in uncertain times*.**
- The status and dominance research (e.g. how a leader's dominance breeds a zero-sum mindset in employees) is relevant when the counterpart is a dominant personality: expect zero-sum framing and counter it with MESOs.
