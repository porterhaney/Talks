# Vermont State-Level Context for Village Wastewater, as It Applies to Charlotte, VT

Research notes compiled 2026-10-10. Dates are given for each finding because the state programs and laws changed a lot between 2019 and 2026. Note on sources: dec.vermont.gov blocks scripted downloads, so some DEC PDFs (for example the full Wastewater Rules text) could not be read in full. Rule specifics below come from a March 2026 DEC presentation to the Legislature and from statute and bill text instead.

## Q1. Why do many Vermont villages lack municipal sewer, and how do on-site septic rules limit density and village growth?

### Takeaway
About two-thirds of Vermont's historic villages have no public wastewater. Growth there depends on each lot's own septic system. Vermont's rules for those systems require at least 36 inches of natural soil above the seasonal high water table (or a mound system), and a 100-foot well-to-septic separation that has been in place since 1949 and can be cut only with a hard site-specific analysis. On small historic village lots, which also need replacement areas, these rules allow roughly one home per lot. Charlotte's villages add a local layer on top: zoning has required 5 acres per dwelling unit, and both villages rely on private wells as well as septic.

### Cited Findings
**Scale and causes**
- In 2015, 219 of Vermont's 335 historic villages, downtowns and hamlets (almost two-thirds) had no public wastewater treatment, per the Agency of Commerce and Community Development — [VTDigger, Dec. 2019 (updated Oct. 2024)](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/)
- Causes cited in that reporting:
  - Many village buildings date from the 1800s and have grandfathered septic systems, and minor upgrades can trigger costly septic replacement.
  - Some villages have poor soils; only one house was built in Westford's village in 50 years.
  - Direct discharge is limited in the Lake Champlain watershed because phosphorus allowances are already allocated.
  - Small towns lack staff to manage complex projects.
  - Residents fear they cannot afford to build or maintain a system.

  Source: [VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/)
- Until 2007, septic systems and wells were regulated town by town. In 2007 Vermont became the last state to set statewide standards. Since then, new homes and failed-system replacements need a state wastewater permit — [VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/). ANR received "universal jurisdiction" over water and wastewater systems from the Legislature in 2007 — [DEC Act 47 report, Jan. 30, 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Reports%20and%20Resources/W~Department%20of%20Environmental%20Conservation~Report%20on%20Municipal%20Water%20and%20Wastewater%20Connections%20Permitting%20and%20Housing~1-30-2025.pdf)
- About 55% of Vermont homes use decentralized (on-site) wastewater, the highest share of any state. Because development density is low, centralized treatment is not feasible for many small towns — [Vermont Legislative Research Service (UVM), "Decentralized Wastewater Management Systems" (c. 2015)](https://www.uvm.edu/d10-files/documents/2024-06/WasteWater.pdf)
- A DEC Village Wastewater Solutions presentation (c. 2022, Lynnette Claudon, PE) gave these reasons:
  - "200+ Vermont villages lack sewage treatment facilities."
  - Most of those villages sit close to rivers, streams or lakes.
  - The lack of treatment "limits opportunities for redevelopment."
  - It makes it hard to establish water-intensive businesses such as restaurants, breweries and food processing.

  Source: [DEC, "Case Studies in Community Wastewater Disposal Systems"](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)

**Technical rules (Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Rules, current version effective Nov. 6, 2023)**
- Vertical separation: the rules require at least 36 inches of natural soil between the leachfield and the seasonal high water table, or a mound system instead. Virus removal happens mainly in this unsaturated zone — [DEC (Larsen and Montross), "Isolation Distances," House Environment, Mar. 25, 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Water%20Quality/W~Ben%20Montross~Water%20and%20Wells%20Presentation%20~3-25-2026.pdf)
- Horizontal separation: "The min. isolation distance of 100ft between septic system and drinking water sources has been in place since 1949."
  - The presumptive isolation zone depends on the size of the wastewater system and the well's pumping rate.
  - It can be reduced only with a 2-year time-of-travel (2YTOT) groundwater analysis, and never below 50 ft for a grouted well or 100 ft for an ungrouted well.
  - DEC calls the analysis "complicated" because most drilled wells draw from fractured bedrock, where water "can move fast and unpredictably."

  Source: [DEC, Mar. 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Water%20Quality/W~Ben%20Montross~Water%20and%20Wells%20Presentation%20~3-25-2026.pdf)
- "Overshadowing": isolation zones extend onto neighboring lots and prohibit activities there. Applicants must notify adjacent landowners when a presumptive isolation zone extends onto abutting lots — [DEC, Mar. 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Water%20Quality/W~Ben%20Montross~Water%20and%20Wells%20Presentation%20~3-25-2026.pdf)
- Soils: "Varved clay [is] common in Champlain Valley" (low permeability). Shallow overburden on bedrock produces a near-surface seasonal high water table — [DEC, Mar. 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Water%20Quality/W~Ben%20Montross~Water%20and%20Wells%20Presentation%20~3-25-2026.pdf)
- Example setbacks from Table 11-1 for siting a water source in bedrock or a confined aquifer: cropland 100 ft; sewer lines 50 ft; stormwater infiltration practices 100 ft. In an unconfined surficial aquifer: cropland 200 ft; sewer lines 75 ft — [DEC, Mar. 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Water%20Quality/W~Ben%20Montross~Water%20and%20Wells%20Presentation%20~3-25-2026.pdf)
- Design flow under the 2019 rules: 2 persons per bedroom for the first three bedrooms, 1 person per additional bedroom, at 70 gallons per person per day. A 3-bedroom home therefore designs at about 420 gpd. I did not confirm this was unchanged in the 2023 rules — [Colchester presentation summarizing the rules](https://www.colchestervt.gov/DocumentCenter/View/4731/GrahameBradleyPresentation)
- Soil-based systems under 6,500 gpd fall under the onsite rules. Systems of 6,500 gpd or more may also need an Indirect Discharge permit, a significantly heavier regulatory tier — [DEC rules archive, Subchapter 5](https://dec.vermont.gov/sites/dec/files/dwgwp/rorulesarchive/pdf/subchap5-6.08.16.02.pdf). Relevant to Charlotte: the West Village municipal system is permitted at 6,499 gpd, just under this threshold — [Charlotte News, Nov. 14, 2019](https://www.charlottenewsvt.org/2019/11/14/flushing-out-the-details-selectboard-moves-closer-to-wastewater-decision/)
- Annualized replacement plus O&M cost of individual on-site systems: conventional $677–971 per year; mound $1,933 per year; innovative/alternative $3,000 per year — [DEC case-study presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)

**Pending reform (not enacted as of the latest record found)**
- H.746 (2026) was introduced by Rep. Olson of Starksboro, Rep. Lalley of Shelburne and others to "enable additional housing development." It would:
  - set a 75-ft minimum isolation distance between wastewater components and potable supplies, unless the Secretary requires more;
  - direct rule changes to cut design flows for compact housing;
  - set standards for compact systems (aerobic treatment units, drip irrigation, and others) and for leachfields under parking lots;
  - require an easement only for the indirect-discharge system itself;
  - exempt leachfields in Class II wetland buffers from wetland permits;
  - exempt village and downtown water and wastewater infrastructure from Act 250.

  Source: [H.746 as introduced](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/BILLS/H-0746/H-0746%20As%20Introduced.pdf). Status: referred to House Environment Jan. 22, 2026, with no later action found — [Bill status](https://legislature.vermont.gov/bill/status/2026/H.746)

**Charlotte specifics tied to these rules**
- CCRPC's 2022 audit found that the village minimum lot area (residential 5 acres per dwelling unit, non-residential 1 acre) "explicitly prevents the development of village." Most historic village lots are under 1 acre. "Wastewater is currently a limiting factor" — [CCRPC memo (Taylor Newton to Larry Lewack), Aug. 29, 2022](https://studiesandreports.ccrpcvt.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Memo_VillageRegsAudit_Charlotte_20220829.pdf)
- Charlotte residents mainly use drilled wells and on-site septic — [DEC Act 47 report, Jan. 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Reports%20and%20Resources/W~Department%20of%20Environmental%20Conservation~Report%20on%20Municipal%20Water%20and%20Wastewater%20Connections%20Permitting%20and%20Housing~1-30-2025.pdf)
- Charlotte's May 2024 village outreach summary found:
  - "Charlotte generally now lacks available community wastewater capacity and water supply in the villages."
  - In the West Village, "some residents fear that allowing any new construction will deplete private wells."
  - "It is not a question if Charlotte's villages will need additional water and wastewater capacity to grow, but how much, and where."

  Source: [Charlotte East & West Villages Project Update #4, May 2024](https://charlottevt.org/vertical/Sites/%7B797C5C63-8973-48DF-A5F9-82DC6FAE7EDF%7D/uploads/VMP_update_4_5.24.pdf)
- Charlotte was one of only two Vermont municipalities, with Colchester, ever to take full delegation of state water and wastewater permitting. Both returned it to ANR, citing administrative and technical review problems, resource limits, and an inability to stay cost-competitive with ANR. ANR also said the records handed back were incomplete — [DEC Act 47 report, Jan. 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Reports%20and%20Resources/W~Department%20of%20Environmental%20Conservation~Report%20on%20Municipal%20Water%20and%20Wastewater%20Connections%20Permitting%20and%20Housing~1-30-2025.pdf). ANR took over Charlotte's wastewater permits, compliance and enforcement on Mar. 1, 2024, after a late-2023 Selectboard vote — [Town of Charlotte](https://charlottevt.org/wastewater)

### Inferences
- In Charlotte, wells and septic constrain growth together. The 100-ft isolation zone around each well and leachfield (each lot also needs a replacement area), together with Champlain Valley clay soils, means the true limit is often the 100-ft circles, not lot size. Shared water would remove many well-septic conflicts. Waitsfield's new water system "eliminated many isolation distance conflicts between private wells and septic systems" ([DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)). So a village water supply may unlock as much density as a sewer.
- The 6,500-gpd threshold matters for system sizing. Expanding the West Village system past 6,499 gpd likely moves it into Indirect Discharge permitting, with more engineering and hydrogeologic study.
- If H.746 or a similar bill passes, cutting the isolation distance from 100 to 75 ft and creating compact-housing design flows would directly raise village lot capacity. It had not advanced as of the latest record found.

### Gaps
- I could not open the current (Nov. 2023) Rules PDF on dec.vermont.gov because the site blocks scripted access. The exact Table 11-2 isolation distances, the replacement-area requirement and the 2023 design-flow table are not verified here.
- I found no estimate of how many units per acre are practically achievable on typical Charlotte village soils without community systems.
- The final 2026 session outcome for H.746 is unconfirmed beyond the January referral.

## Q2. Which state programs fund village wastewater, and has Charlotte received any awards?

### Takeaway
The main dedicated program was DEC's ARPA-funded Village Water and Wastewater Initiative (about $30M). All its money was obligated by Dec. 31, 2024, with a spend deadline of 2026, so it is effectively closed. Twelve villages were funded; four dropped out, Westford among them. What remains:
- the CWSRF (with planning-loan subsidies),
- Engineering Planning Advances (repaid only if the project is built),
- Pollution Control Grants,
- USDA Rural Development (income-tested, so Charlotte is likely ineligible),
- the Northern Border Regional Commission,
- the new Act 69 (2025) CHIP tax-increment tool, launched January 2026,
- a roughly $9M housing-infrastructure revolving loan fund (2025).

DEC itself says existing water and wastewater funding programs "are not designed to accommodate growth." I found no state wastewater construction award to Charlotte.

### Cited Findings
**ARPA Village Water and Wastewater Initiative**
- The governor released "nearly $30 million" for village water and wastewater infrastructure in October 2022. The round funded 12 villages (11 wastewater, 1 drinking water) on a "co-funding" model alongside the CWSRF and DWSRF, USDA-RD, the NBRC and local funds — [Governor's press release, Oct. 26, 2022](https://governor.vermont.gov/press-release/governor-phil-scott-announces-release-nearly-30-million-village-water-and-wastewater); [VTDigger, Jan. 2023](https://vtdigger.org/2023/01/25/covid-funds-buoy-wastewater-projects-statewide/)
- ARPA funds had to be obligated by Dec. 31, 2024 and spent by the end of 2026 — [Chester Telegraph, Nov. 2022](https://www.chestertelegraph.org/2022/11/01/3-area-villages-to-get-arpa-funds-for-wastewater-projects/). DEC's March 2024 letter to Westford used a spend-by date of Sept. 30, 2026 — [Westford ARPA rescission letter, Mar. 19, 2024](https://westfordvt.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/20240319-Westford_ARPA_Recission_Letter.pdf)
- Status as of May 20, 2025:
  - Twelve projects were supported.
  - Four advanced through planning and then ceased: Westford, Grafton, Greensboro and Moretown.
  - Eight continue through design and construction: Highgate, North and South Londonderry, Montgomery, Wolcott, Waitsfield, Burke and Killington.
  - None are in Chittenden County apart from Westford, which ceased.

  Source: [N. Kamman, DEC ARPA overview to House Appropriations, May 20, 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Appropriations/Other%20Topics/ARPA/W~Neil%20Kamman~DEC%20ARPA%20Overview~5-20-2025.pdf)
- Other DEC ARPA lines:
  - Healthy Homes on-site program: 624 awards to low-income homeowners for failed septic systems and wells.
  - Municipal Pollution Control Grant supplements: 5 awards, including **Hinesburg**.
  - Rural Infrastructure: $10M.

  Source: [Kamman, May 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Appropriations/Other%20Topics/ARPA/W~Neil%20Kamman~DEC%20ARPA%20Overview~5-20-2025.pdf)
- The program page states: "While this program has passed, municipalities are encouraged to apply for financial support should additional funding become available" — [ANR Village Water and Wastewater Initiative](https://anr.vermont.gov/content/village-water-wastewater-initiative)

**Ongoing DEC programs**
- Programs listed on DEC's funding page:
  - Clean Water State Revolving Fund (planning, design and construction loans)
  - Engineering Planning Advance (state planning loan)
  - Pollution Control Grants (state construction grant)
  - Municipal Planning Grant (ACCD)
  - Northern Border Regional Commission
  - USDA-RD SEARCH and Water and Environmental Program grants and loans
  - On-site Loan Program
  - Clean Water Fund / Clean Water Initiative Program

  Source: [DEC Village Wastewater Solutions Finance and Guidance](https://dec.vermont.gov/village-wastewater/funding-guidance)
- Program terms from DEC's c. 2022 presentation:
  - Engineering Planning Advance: "No caps"; first come, first served; "Repayment only if the project goes to construction."
  - CWSRF planning: "100% subsidy up to the cap, $125k," with repayment of the amount over the cap in 5 years.
  - Affordability benchmark: annual user rate of 1–2% of median household income.

  Source: [DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)
- DEC's Act 47 stakeholder report (Jan. 30, 2025): "Existing funding programs are not designed to accommodate growth. Projects to address capacity limitations or to extend services to accommodate new housing development are often low priority or ineligible for existing water and wastewater funding programs." It also says that addressing capacity "will facilitate and promote housing development to a much greater extent than modifying the municipal connections permitting framework," and that "ANR does not track municipal wastewater capacity" — [DEC Act 47 report](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Environment/Reports%20and%20Resources/W~Department%20of%20Environmental%20Conservation~Report%20on%20Municipal%20Water%20and%20Wastewater%20Connections%20Permitting%20and%20Housing~1-30-2025.pdf)

**Housing laws that interact with wastewater**
- Act 47 (HOME Act, 2023):
  - In areas served by municipal sewer and water, bylaws must allow 5 or more units per acre for each allowed residential use, plus a 40% density bonus for affordable housing.
  - "Served" means residential connections to municipal water and wastewater systems are "available." ACCD noted that "available" is undefined.

  Sources: [Act 47 as enacted](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2024/Docs/ACTS/ACT047/ACT047%20As%20Enacted.pdf); [ACCD preliminary summary](https://www.town.williston.vt.us/vertical/sites/%7BF506B13C-605B-4878-8062-87E5927E49F0%7D/uploads/DHCD-Planning-Act47-PreliminarySummary-v2.pdf)
- The HOME Act requires areas served by water and sewer to allow triplexes and fourplexes. Act 181 (2024) exempts certain housing in water- and sewer-served areas from Act 250. VLCT's Samantha Sheehan called the new housing laws "just on paper" without infrastructure — [VTDigger, Feb. 25, 2025](https://vtdigger.org/2025/02/25/to-add-housing-in-much-of-vermont-you-need-wastewater-infrastructure-local-opposition-can-kill-it/)
- Act 152 (2026, S.325) amends Act 181. It extends interim Act 250 housing exemptions to Jan. 1, 2028. One exemption covers up to 50 units on 10 acres or less within a designated village center, and within a quarter mile of its boundary, that has permanent zoning and subdivision bylaws and is "served by public sewer or water services or soils that are adequate for wastewater disposal."
  - Tier 1B areas get an Act 250 exemption for 50 or fewer housing units on 10 acres or less.
  - The regional "village area" future-land-use category requires that "the municipality has either municipal public water or wastewater. If no public wastewater is available, the area must have soils that are adequate for wastewater disposal."

  Source: [Act 152 as enacted](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/ACTS/ACT152/ACT152%20As%20Enacted.pdf)
- Act 69 (2025, S.127) created the Community and Housing Infrastructure Program (CHIP), a project-based tax-increment tool for housing infrastructure, clean water included.
  - VEPC may approve up to $200M a year in education-tax increment.
  - Retention runs up to 20 years, at up to 75% of the education increment plus 10 points for affordability.
  - The program launched in January 2026, and Middlebury is an early user.

  Sources: [Act 69 summary](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/ACTS/ACT069/ACT069%20Act%20Summary.pdf); [VLCT CHIP FAQ](https://www.vlct.org/node/4303); [JFO fiscal note](https://ljfo.vermont.gov/assets/Publications/2025-2026-Senate-Bills/GENERAL-381569-v9-Act69_FN-v2.pdf); [States Newsroom/Addison Independent, 2026](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/middlebury-taps-new-housing-development-tool)
- The 2025 housing omnibus included about $9M for a low-interest revolving loan fund for housing infrastructure, plus TIF tools for smaller towns. Housing Commissioner Alex Farrell said developers often build their own infrastructure and pass the cost to buyers — [VTDigger, Feb. 2025](https://vtdigger.org/2025/02/25/to-add-housing-in-much-of-vermont-you-need-wastewater-infrastructure-local-opposition-can-kill-it/)

**Charlotte-specific funding found**
- CCRPC allocated $72,000 to Charlotte for the two-year East and West Villages planning project (Dubois & King; September 2023 to about April 2025) — [Town of Charlotte "Planning for Our Future" page](https://charlottevt.org/?SEC=9A0002B4-4B04-4BEA-92DE-97ADA34A1D87); [Charlotte News, Aug. 8, 2024](https://www.charlottenewsvt.org/2024/08/08/finally-a-look-at-east-and-west-village-project-update/)
- Charlotte's West Village municipal system was built with town money in 2001 (excess capacity "developed with taxpayer money in 2001"). It has a 6,499-gpd permit, and the site could eventually support about 15,000 gpd — [Charlotte News, Nov. 14, 2019](https://www.charlottenewsvt.org/2019/11/14/flushing-out-the-details-selectboard-moves-closer-to-wastewater-decision/)
- I found no ARPA village wastewater award, CWSRF construction loan or Pollution Control Grant to Charlotte. Charlotte does not appear on DEC's May 2025 ARPA project list — [Kamman, May 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Appropriations/Other%20Topics/ARPA/W~Neil%20Kamman~DEC%20ARPA%20Overview~5-20-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- The big grant window (ARPA) has closed, and Charlotte did not use it. A future Charlotte project would most likely be built from:
  - EPA or CWSRF planning money with subsidy,
  - CWSRF construction loans,
  - a possible Pollution Control Grant,
  - NBRC funds (Chittenden County eligibility should be checked),
  - possibly CHIP or TIF tied to specific housing projects.

  Grant shares would be lower than in the ARPA-era projects.
- Charlotte's high median income likely rules out USDA-RD grants. Westford, a neighboring Chittenden town, was not USDA-eligible at an MHI of $91K ([DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)). The 1–2%-of-MHI affordability test is easier for a high-income town to meet, but it also means less subsidy.
- Act 152's Act 250 village exemption accepts "soils adequate for wastewater disposal" in place of sewer. In theory, Charlotte's villages could qualify on soils alone, but only if they hold designated village center status and have permanent bylaws. Whether Charlotte holds Village Center designation should be verified.

### Gaps
- I did not research the Vermont Housing and Conservation Board (VHCB) or the Vermont Housing Finance Agency (VHFA) role in funding wastewater for housing projects. VHCB funds affordable-housing developments, and their on-site or shared infrastructure costs may be eligible, but I found no source.
- I did not confirm Charlotte's Village Center designation status, or whether CCRPC's regional future land use map (under Act 181 and Act 152) maps Charlotte's villages as "village areas" or Tier 1B.
- I did not establish whether ARPA dollars Westford and Moretown returned were re-awarded, or whether any new DEC village wastewater round is planned for FY2027.
- NBRC eligibility of Chittenden County is unverified.

## Q3. Lake Champlain phosphorus TMDL context relevant to Charlotte

### Takeaway
The 2016 EPA phosphorus TMDL for Lake Champlain sets wasteload allocations for 59 Vermont treatment plants and assigns most of the required reductions to agriculture, stormwater and other nonpoint sources. That makes a new direct-discharge sewer plant very hard to permit in the basin. In practice, new village systems in the Champlain basin are soil-based (indirect discharge) systems. Charlotte sits on the lake between the Shelburne Bay segment (LaPlatte) and the Main Lake. The Hinesburg plant upstream on the LaPlatte had to cut phosphorus about 75%, which drove a roughly $12.5M rebuild.

### Cited Findings
- The 2016 EPA TMDL sets annual phosphorus wasteload allocations for 59 wastewater treatment facilities in the Vermont basin. Tighter reductions apply only where wastewater makes up at least 10% of a lake segment's phosphorus load — [DEC TMDL page](https://dec.vermont.gov/node/2663); [EPA, Phosphorus TMDLs for Vermont Segments of Lake Champlain, June 17, 2016](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-06/documents/phosphorus-tmdls-vermont-segments-lake-champlain-jun-17-2016.pdf)
- Plants above 0.1 MGD discharging to the Main Lake segment get allocations based on 0.8 mg/L phosphorus (0.1–0.2 MGD) or 0.2 mg/L (above 0.2 MGD) at permitted flow — [ANR WWTF TMDL FAQ](https://anr.vermont.gov/sites/anr/files/documents/ww_WWTF-TMDL_FAQ.pdf)
- Example of a small Main Lake plant: Richmond was assigned 0.061 metric tons per year (about 134 lb/yr) — [Richmond response summary](https://www.richmondvt.gov/fileadmin/files/Archive/2019/04/3b-3-1173.Response.Summary.20201016.pdf)
- 2015 EPA proposal tables showed agriculture reductions of 49.1% for the Main Lake and 22.2% for Shelburne Bay, with Shelburne Bay showing a 55.0% figure in another column, likely developed land. These are proposal-stage figures with a hard-to-read layout — [EPA Aug. 2015 public meeting presentation](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2015-09/documents/aug-2015-public-meeting-presentation.pdf)
- Direct discharge is limited in the Lake Champlain watershed because phosphorus allowances are already allocated. DEC's Lynnette Claudon: "There's a lot of limitations with direct discharge wastewater systems" — [VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/)
- Hinesburg (LaPlatte River):
  - A 2018 discharge permit required cuts to phosphorus and ammonia discharges to the LaPlatte. The consultant said the state required about a 75% phosphorus reduction, which the lagoon could not meet.
  - Voters approved a bond of up to $11.7M, and a $12.5M bid (Naylor and Breen) was accepted after earlier bids over $15M were rejected.
  - Permitted capacity was to rise from 250,000 to 325,000 gpd.

  Sources: [The Citizen (vtcng), bond approved, 2020](https://www.vtcng.com/thecitizenvt/news/local_news_hinesburg/new-wastewater-treatment-facility-bond-approved/article_01c45590-1f98-11eb-b0b0-af94d2f53166.html); [The Citizen, bid accepted, 2024](https://www.vtcng.com/thecitizenvt/news/local_news_hinesburg/hinesburg-accepts-wastewater-plant-bid/article_e6bf382e-0322-11ef-8402-f74f730e79bc.html); [ANR permit application 2022](https://anrweb.vt.gov/Pubdocs/DEC/ENB/WWINV/17493-3-1172_Renewal-Amend%20Appl.20220428.pdf)
- VEPC staff (March 2023) tied Hinesburg's added 75,000 gpd to an estimated 300+ perpetually affordable homes, including pipeline projects of 20 and 60 affordable units — [VEPC minutes, Mar. 30, 2023](https://outside.vermont.gov/agency/ACCD/ACCD_Web_Docs/ED/VEPC/2023/VEPCMeetingMinutes3.30.2023.pdf)
- The Northern Lake Champlain Direct Drainages Tactical Basin Plan (December 2020) covers the direct drainages from about Charlotte and Shelburne north, and prioritizes agricultural phosphorus reduction — [DEC TBP (via search summary)](https://dec.vermont.gov/sites/dec/files/WID/WPP/TBP5finalSigned.pdf). (I could not open the PDF to confirm Charlotte-specific content.)
- An update to the Lake Champlain TMDL is anticipated around 2026 — [Warren County NY Watershed Implementation Plan, 2023](https://public.warrencountyny.gov/gis/PlanLibrary/2023%20Lake%20Champlain%20TMDL%20Watershed%20Implementation%20Plan.pdf)
- A failed septic system can release large volumes of untreated sewage, carrying nitrogen, phosphorus and pathogens to ground and surface water — [VLRS/UVM](https://www.uvm.edu/d10-files/documents/2024-06/WasteWater.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Charlotte, the TMDL is less a direct barrier (the town has no discharging plant) than a reason any community solution would be soil-based (leachfield or indirect discharge), not a lake-discharging plant. This also explains why sewering Charlotte's villages to a regional plant is unattractive: Hinesburg's own LaPlatte limits drove its costly rebuild.
- Shared village systems with managed pumping and advanced treatment can be framed as a water-quality improvement over aging individual septic systems near Thorp Brook, Lewis Creek, LaPlatte tributaries and the lake. Warren's project grew directly out of contamination data from the Friends of the Mad River.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain segment-level TMDL allocations for the Shelburne Bay, Main Lake or Otter Creek segments that cover Charlotte, nor Charlotte-specific tactical basin plan actions for Thorp Brook, Lewis Creek or the LaPlatte.
- I found no data on septic failure rates or E. coli monitoring in Charlotte's villages (for example, from the Lewis Creek Association or South Chittenden River Watch).
- I did not confirm whether Hinesburg's new plant has been commissioned (expected around 2025).

## Q4. Comparable Vermont case studies: costs, capacity, outcomes and lessons

### Takeaway
The successful Vermont model is decentralized:
- septic-tank effluent collection (STEP or STEG) to one or a few shared community leachfields,
- incremental growth,
- an engaged local wastewater committee,
- a municipal operator,
- grants stacked on loans,
- user rates near 1–2% of median household income.

Warren is the template: 30,000 gpd, about 85–95 properties, $4.66M, $1,180 per year in 2020. Failures are mostly political or physical. Westford voters rejected its bond in 2023; Moretown found no suitable soils; Greensboro lost its site; Waitsfield's earlier bond failed; Montgomery's costs ballooned and an opt-out petition followed.

### Cited Findings
**Warren (Mad River Valley), the main success model**
- 1997: a 5,000-gpd cluster system at Brooks Field (school field), funded with the Pitcher Inn's support, served the Inn, the Warren Store, the fire station, the post office, town offices and two homes.
- 1999–2000: EPA Special Demonstration Grant.
- Construction May 2003 to October 2004:
  - septic-tank effluent collection,
  - the Brooks Field leachfield expanded to 30,000 gpd,
  - an additional 2,000-gpd shared system,
  - 10 individual on-site systems upgraded.
- Total cost $4,662,000, funded by an EPA Demonstration Grant, an EPA STAG grant, a Vermont pollution abatement grant and match, and an SRF loan. 2020 user fee: $1,180 per year; debt service is on the grand list.
- Run by a village employee, a contract operator and engineer, and a sewer commission.
- A Wastewater Advisory Committee did door-to-door surveys and offered free well tests. Many tests showed contamination, and "most homeowners voluntarily signed up."

  Source: [DEC/ECO AmeriCorps Warren case study, 2020](https://dec.vermont.gov/sites/dec/files/WID/Warren,%20VT%20Wastewater%20Solutions_finalCaseStudy.pdf)
- DEC's slide summary of Warren: 10 individual refurbishments, 1 cluster serving 3 properties, 78 properties on a large shared system, and alternative treatment at the school — [DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf). VTDigger cited 95 properties on two offsite systems and annual fees of $450–700 (2019) — [VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/). Property counts and fees differ by source and year.
- EPA has its own Warren case study, "A Different Approach for Managing..." — [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-06/documents/warren_report_1.pdf)

**Other DEC-documented villages** (all from [DEC presentation, c. 2022](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf))
- Brownsville (West Windsor, 2016): bought the bankrupt Ascutney ski area's sewer system with a CWSRF loan after Irene damaged village septic systems, then built a village collection system. Debt is split between the grand list and user fees; user fee $865 per year. VTDigger noted a general store reopened and added a restaurant ([VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/)).
- Rochester (2018): 3 community soil-based sites plus 1 reserve, originally built in the 1970s at 66,150 gpd to end straight pipes. Rebuilding the disposal field cost $499,000 in total ($463,072 construction), all CWSRF-funded; user fee $340 per year.
- Grafton: a sewer feasibility study estimated $3–4M, so the town instead adopted a village septic pump-out ordinance (about $285 every five years, with proof of pumping). The town "still concerned with ability to grow or redevelop village properties with small lots."
- Waitsfield: a new drinking-water system "eliminated many isolation distance conflicts between private wells and septic systems" ($628 per ERU per year, 2022). An earlier community wastewater bond vote failed, leaving almost $1M in planning and design loans to repay. Waitsfield is now an ARPA project: about $18.5M estimate, 89,000-gpd disposal at the Munn site, with 100% grant and loan funding sought ([Waitsfield project FAQ](https://www.waitsfieldvt.gov/departments/projects/wastewater/faq)).
- Montgomery (pre-inflation figures): 192 connections in two villages, about $11.8M. Funding: USDA-RD grant of about $6M, USDA-RD loan of about $5M, CWSRF subsidy of $0.25M, and ARPA of about $0.5M. Repayment from user fees alone would be $1,450 per year, or 3.4% of MHI, so the town added impact fees and a local option tax.

**Westford (Chittenden County), the cautionary case closest to Charlotte**
- DEC's planning figures:
  - Total project cost $3.3M, including a $15K Engineering Planning Advance, about $18K in Municipal Planning Grant money, a $300K land purchase, and the rest as a CWSRF loan.
  - Westford was not USDA-RD-eligible (MHI $91K).
  - User rate would be $1,638 per year at 100% loan, $1,180 at 65% loan, or $754 at 50% loan (1.7%, 1.3% or 0.8% of MHI).

  Source: [DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)
- Planned to serve 50–60 residences; acquired land for a 7-acre leach field. Town official Melissa Manka: "Westford has a vision but without wastewater, it's just a vision" — [VTDigger 2019](https://vtdigger.org/2019/12/12/lack-of-public-wastewater-systems-inhibits-rural-development/)
- Awarded $2,377,000 in ARPA for construction (early 2022) — [Westford minutes, Feb. 7, 2022](https://westfordvt.us/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/February-7-2022-Minutes.pdf)
- In November 2023, voters rejected a $400,000 bond 488–532. Opponents felt the project did not suit the town and feared new development — [WCAX, Nov. 8, 2023](https://www.wcax.com/2023/11/08/westford-residents-vote-down-new-wastewater-treatment-system)
- The project was paused and a Wastewater Alternatives Committee formed — [Westford ARPA report, Mar. 31, 2024](https://westfordvt.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Biannual-ARPA-WW-Report-03-31-2024.pdf). The town returned its unspent federal grant — [VTDigger, Feb. 2025](https://vtdigger.org/2025/02/25/to-add-housing-in-much-of-vermont-you-need-wastewater-infrastructure-local-opposition-can-kill-it/). DEC lists it as "ceased" — [Kamman, May 2025](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/House%20Appropriations/Other%20Topics/ARPA/W~Neil%20Kamman~DEC%20ARPA%20Overview~5-20-2025.pdf)

**Wolcott (Lamoille), a first-ever sewer**
- About $4.4M cost, with more than $5M in state and federal funds lined up, including $750K from the NBRC plus ARPA, expected to cover the full cost.
- Individual septic tanks feed a pump station and a community leach field near the school, designed for a 500-year flood, with about 50 School Street properties.
- The country store owner said the septic was "just not big enough to support the store and all the apartments." A regional planner: "wastewater is the foundation."

  Source: [VTDigger, Oct. 24, 2024](https://vtdigger.org/2024/10/24/wolcott-poised-to-build-its-first-sewer-system-aiming-for-development-and-flood-resiliency/)

**Montgomery, Moretown, Greensboro: political and site lessons (2025)**
- Montgomery's cost rose from about $12M to $16M and the scope was halved after a landowner withdrew. A 100+ signature petition forced votes on charter changes allowing opt-outs, which the selectboard chair said could doom the project. A planning chair said, "Nobody moved here for a more dense population."
- Moretown returned its grant after soil tests found none of four candidate disposal sites feasible.
- Greensboro lost its site when a church withdrew.
- DEC's Claudon: "It's not uncommon for people in our communities to not like change."

  Source: [VTDigger, Feb. 25, 2025](https://vtdigger.org/2025/02/25/to-add-housing-in-much-of-vermont-you-need-wastewater-infrastructure-local-opposition-can-kill-it/)

**DEC's "Keys to Successful Projects"**
1. A strong wastewater committee.
2. Visioning, meaning complementary planning and economic studies done first.
3. Capacity, meaning a staff person to run the system.
4. Grant funding on top of loans.
5. Affordable user rates.

Source: [DEC presentation](https://www.westminstervt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Case-Studies-in-Community-Wastewater-Disposal-Systems.pdf)

**Hinesburg (Charlotte's neighbor; centralized plant upgrade, not a village startup)**
- About $12.5M plant rebuild for LaPlatte phosphorus limits, raising capacity from 250,000 to 325,000 gpd, linked to 300+ affordable homes. See the Q3 citations above.

### Inferences
- Charlotte's own West Village system already follows the Warren model in miniature: a town-owned shared soil-based system serving public buildings, with ordinances adopted in 2019. The same incremental "cluster first, expand later" path is the one DEC's case studies support.
- Westford is Charlotte's closest analog: an affluent Chittenden County town, no USDA eligibility, ARPA money in hand, rural-character politics. It shows that the deciding risk is local votes, not engineering. Charlotte's outreach found only 3–10% of respondents opposed any village growth, but the town describes its reputation as "hostile to development" ([Charlotte update #4](https://charlottevt.org/vertical/Sites/%7B797C5C63-8973-48DF-A5F9-82DC6FAE7EDF%7D/uploads/VMP_update_4_5.24.pdf)). Opt-in versus mandatory connection is a known make-or-break design choice.
- Waitsfield's experience suggests that a shared village water supply could ease well-septic isolation conflicts, which is directly relevant to West Village residents' fears about their wells.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable figures on specific community systems in Huntington, Jeffersonville, Stowe, Craftsbury, Monkton, Hartland or Shrewsbury. The searches did not return them. Jeffersonville's known constraint is a state moratorium on new water connections since 2018, not wastewater ([search summary of Vermont Rural Water material](https://vtruralwater.org/water-sewer-housing/)), and that is unverified in the primary source.
- Williston and Stowe have conventional municipal sewer and are not good analogs. I did not research them.
- Current 2026 status of Wolcott, Montgomery and Waitsfield construction is unconfirmed.

## Q5. What does the Chittenden County Regional Planning Commission (CCRPC) say about Charlotte's village wastewater?

### Takeaway
CCRPC's main Charlotte-specific statement is its August 2022 audit of village regulations. It calls wastewater "currently a limiting factor." It says the 5-acre per-unit minimum lot size "explicitly prevents the development of village." It recommends:
- removing residential density caps so density is limited by building form and on-site wastewater capacity,
- merging the East and West village districts,
- shrinking lot sizes and frontages,
- doing village planning that includes wastewater planning before more zoning changes.

CCRPC then sat on, and helped fund, the 2023–2025 East and West Villages project.

### Cited Findings
- From the CCRPC audit (Taylor Newton, Senior Planner, to Charlotte Town Planner Larry Lewack, Aug. 29, 2022):
  - Recommends village planning (a "character assessment" plus visioning) to guide "wastewater planning, public investment."
  - The East and West village districts are "nearly identical" and should be merged into one "Village District."
  - Keeping the Commercial/Light Industrial district "makes sense if there ends up being a municipal wastewater system... However, if Charlotte does not intend to develop a municipal wastewater system then it makes little sense to have a zoning district dedicated to primarily industrial uses."
  - Minimum lot area (5 acres per residential unit, 1 acre non-residential) "explicitly prevents the development of village." Most historic lots are under 1 acre.
  - "Most local zoning in Chittenden County municipalities allow for a density of between 2 dwelling units (in unsewered areas) and 12 dwelling units per acre if sewered." Transit needs at least 5 units per acre.
  - "Wastewater is currently a limiting factor, but there is no practical reason to restrict density in zoning so that the Village can evolve over time and provide developers flexibility before a public wastewater system is provided (if ever)."
  - Recommends removing all residential density standards so density "would also be restricted by the ability of a property to treat wastewater on-site," and reducing frontage from 150 ft to 75 ft or less.

  Source: [CCRPC memo](https://studiesandreports.ccrpcvt.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Memo_VillageRegsAudit_Charlotte_20220829.pdf)
- CCRPC sat on the East and West Village project steering committee with Dubois & King. The July 11, 2024 presentation laid out "various options for wastewater disposal and water supply in the villages, from smaller, localized options to larger municipal systems" — [Charlotte News, Aug. 8, 2024](https://www.charlottenewsvt.org/2024/08/08/finally-a-look-at-east-and-west-village-project-update/). CCRPC's FY25 annual report for Charlotte lists the project — [CCRPC FY25 Charlotte report](https://www.ccrpcvt.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Charlotte_CCRPC_FY25_Final.pdf)
- The 2026 Charlotte Town Plan Appendix C (hosted by CCRPC, December 2025) references the 2011 "Final Report on Potential Community Wastewater Service to the West Charlotte Village." That report found "sufficient in-ground capacity available to expand the existing wastewater system," and it led to a small public West Village system "currently restricted to commercial users." A 2016 follow-up report on expanding service is also listed — [CCRPC-hosted Appendix C, 2026 Charlotte Town Plan](https://www.ccrpcvt.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2026CharlotteTownPlan_AppendixC_OtherPlans_reduced.pdf) (from the search summary; the PDF was not fully read)

### Inferences
- CCRPC's position is to decouple zoning from wastewater: let on-site capacity be the binding constraint and stop stacking a 5-acre rule on top. That positions Charlotte to benefit if and when shared capacity arrives.
- The 2026 Town Plan (hosted by CCRPC in December 2025) suggests Charlotte adopted or was adopting a new plan in 2026. Its wastewater language should be checked by whoever covers Charlotte-local documents.

### Gaps
- I did not find a CCRPC wastewater feasibility study specific to Charlotte, and I did not check how CCRPC's regional plan future land use map (Act 181 and Act 152 process, 2025–2026) classifies Charlotte's East and West villages (village area, Tier 1B, or other).
- I did not read the full text of the 2024–2025 Dubois & King village concept plan's wastewater options (costs, gpd, sites). It is likely on the town's "Planning for Our Future" page.
