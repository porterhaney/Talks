You already use AI

Here's the first secret: you've been using artificial intelligence for years. Nobody announced it. It just showed up inside things you already own.

  • Your iPhone unlocking when it sees your face. That's AI recognizing you, glasses or no glasses, bad hair day or not.
  • Typing "dog" into the Photos app and getting every picture of the dog. Nobody labeled those. The phone figured it out.
  • Autocorrect guessing the word you meant. (And sometimes the word you absolutely did not mean.)
  • Your spam folder, quietly catching the Nigerian princes.
  • Your Facebook feed. AI decides which posts you see first, and which ones will keep you scrolling.

So what is AI, really?

Here's the simplest definition I know: AI is a computer doing something that used to require a person's judgment. Recognizing a face. Understanding a sentence. Deciding if an email is junk.

The interesting part is how it learns to do that. Old-fashioned software follows rules a programmer wrote down, step by step, like a recipe card. AI learns from examples instead.

The grandkid test

How did you teach your kids what a dog is? You didn't hand them a rulebook. "Four legs, fur, a tail" also describes the cat, and the cat would be offended. You just pointed at dogs. Lots of dogs. Eventually they got it, and they could spot a dog they'd never seen before.

That's machine learning, the engine under almost all modern AI. Show a computer a million examples and let it work out the pattern on its own.

Everything in the rest of this book, ChatGPT included, is that same idea scaled up to an almost silly size. The computer didn't look at a million dog pictures. It read a meaningful fraction of everything humans have ever written down.

Keep it a tool

Your Facebook feed is built to keep you scrolling. A chatbot only talks when you ask. Pick it up with a question, put it down when you have the answer. It makes a great research assistant and a lousy friend: it agrees with everything and has never once bought a round. Save the real conversations for Frank and Joanne, Clarence, and us.

Where it came from

AI feels like it arrived in 2022. It actually has a 75-year backstory, with a couple of long naps in the middle.

WhenWhat happened
1950Alan Turing, the British codebreaker, asks "Can machines think?" His test: if you can't tell whether you're chatting with a person or a machine, call it thinking.
1956A summer workshop at Dartmouth, in Hanover, New Hampshire, coins the phrase "artificial intelligence." The organizers figured one summer would get them most of the way. It did not.
1966ELIZA, a simple MIT program, plays therapist by repeating your words back as questions. People confide in it anyway. Its creator's secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private.
1970s–80sThe "AI winters." Big promises, small results, and the research money froze. Twice.
1997–2011IBM's Deep Blue beats chess champion Garry Kasparov. Later, IBM's Watson wins Jeopardy! Alex Trebek remains unimpressed.
2012Computers get good at recognizing photos by studying over a million labeled examples on chips built for video games. The modern approach starts working.
2016Google's AlphaGo beats one of the world's best players at Go, a board game with more possible positions than atoms in the universe.
2017Eight Google researchers publish a paper introducing the "transformer," a better recipe for teaching computers language. It's the T in ChatGPT.
Nov 2022ChatGPT goes public. It reportedly hits 100 million users in about two months, the fastest-growing app anyone had seen.
2023–nowEveryone piles in. Chatbots learn to see photos, listen, talk out loud, and lately, do tasks for you. Those are the "agents" in Chapter 6.

Why did it all happen now?

The core ideas are decades old. Three ingredients finally showed up at the same time:

  1. Reading material. The internet gave computers billions of pages of human writing to learn from.
  2. Horsepower. Those video game chips (mostly from a company called Nvidia) turned out to be perfect for the math.
  3. A better recipe. The 2017 transformer idea made learning from all that text practical.

Like bread: people knew how to bake for centuries. What was missing was enough flour and an oven the size of a warehouse. Literally. More on the warehouses in Chapter 5.

How ChatGPT works

Finish this sentence: "Peanut butter and ____." You said jelly. You didn't look it up. You've just seen those words together a thousand times.

That, believe it or not, is the heart of ChatGPT. It is a machine that is extraordinarily good at guessing the next word. Your iPhone does a tiny version when it suggests the next word above the keyboard. ChatGPT is that trick after reading a library the size of a country.

School: how it learns

First, the AI goes through "training." Picture the biggest fill-in-the-blank quiz in history. The computer reads a sentence from its pile of books and websites, hides the next word, guesses, and checks the answer. When it's wrong, it adjusts itself a tiny bit. Then it does that again. Trillions of times, for months, on warehouses full of computers.

The "adjusting" happens on what engineers call parameters. Picture a sound mixing board with hundreds of billions of little knobs. Every wrong guess nudges some knobs. When training is done, the knobs are locked, and that knob setting is the AI. When you ask it something, it writes a fresh answer one word at a time, which is why the same question can get a slightly different answer twice.

The surprising part

To guess the next word in a chemistry textbook, it helps to pick up some chemistry. To guess the next line of a French novel, it helps to learn French. Nobody programmed grammar, facts or sonnets into ChatGPT. They soaked in as a side effect of getting very good at the guessing game, which surprised even the people who built it.

Finishing school: learning manners

Having read the whole internet, it has also read the worst of it. So in a second round, real people grade its answers for helpfulness, politeness and safety. It learns to act like an assistant, refuse dangerous requests, and admit when it doesn't know. (In theory. See Chapter 4.)

Decoding the letters

LLM = Large Language Model. Large because it's enormous. Language because it works with words. Model means the finished product.

GPT = Generative Pre-trained Transformer. It generates writing, it was trained in advance, and it runs on the 2017 transformer recipe. Engineers should never name things.

The big one: "hallucination"

You'll see this word in every article about AI. It means the AI stated something false with total confidence. It might invent a book, author and publisher included, that does not exist.

Why does that happen? Remember Chapter 3: the machine is built to produce the most likely-sounding next word. Usually likely-sounding and true are the same thing. Sometimes they aren't, and the machine can't always tell the difference. It isn't lying. It has no idea it's wrong.

The best way to think about it

Treat it like a brilliant, well-read houseguest who has never once in their life said "I don't know." Fascinating at dinner. Wonderful for ideas. You would still double-check before you let them do your taxes.

Four rules of thumb

  1. If it matters, verify it. Health, money and legal things get checked with a real person or a real source.
  2. Ask "where did you get that?" Then check that the source actually exists.
  3. Push back. "Are you sure?" often gets a more careful answer. It won't sulk.
  4. Be specific. "Write a birthday note" gets you a greeting card. "A funny, warm note for my sister who just turned 70 and loves gardening" gets you something worth sending.

For the record, I checked every fact in this booklet. The AI's first draft was pretty good. "Pretty good" is why you check.

"Open" versus "closed"

This trips up everyone, so don't feel bad. OpenAI is just a company name. Ironically, its best AI is closed: you can use it, but you can't take it home. Anthropic and Google work the same way.

Other companies give their AI away for anyone to download and run on their own computers. That's called open source or open weights. Meta's Llama, DeepSeek from China and Mistral from France are the names you'll see.

Think of it like restaurants

A closed AI is a restaurant: you order, the food comes out, the kitchen stays private. An open AI is a published recipe: anyone can cook it at home, tweak it, or open a competing restaurant. Both have fans, and the argument fills a lot of articles.

Why is it in the news every single day?

Money. The big tech companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on "data centers," which are warehouses packed with those Nvidia chips, plus the power plants to run them. Nvidia became the most valuable company in the world selling those chips.

Is it a bubble? Probably partly. The railroads were a bubble too, and we still got the railroads. Expect headlines about both the bubble and the railroads for years.

Agents: AI that does things

A chatbot talks. An agent does. That's the whole difference, and it's why every article in 2026 has the word "agent" in it.

Ask a chatbot how to book a flight and it lists the steps. Ask an agent and it opens the airline website, compares fares and fills in the forms. One friend gives you directions to the airport. The other drives you there. In your car. With your credit card.

How an agent works

An agent is the same kind of AI from Chapter 3, running in a loop and given some "tools," which are its hands: a web browser, your email, your calendar, a shopping site. It goes around and around:

LookDecideActCheck the resultRepeat

Say you tell one: "Book a table for four at a good Italian place near our hotel, Saturday at 7." It searches restaurants, reads reviews, opens the reservation page, picks the time, fills in your name, and (if it's a well-built one) stops to ask you before it hits "confirm."

When the article says "open AI agent"

It's usually one of three things:

1. OpenAI's agent. The ChatGPT company launched "ChatGPT agent" in July 2025. It works inside ChatGPT and can click around websites for you.

2. An open-source agent, most likely OpenClaw. It's free, people install it on their own computers, and it went viral in January 2026 as the fastest-growing project ever on GitHub (a giant library of software). It also became a security headache almost immediately. Hold that thought.

3. Just "agentic AI" in general, meaning any AI that takes actions on its own. "Agentic" is the favorite word of people with a slide deck.

Why the excitement, and why the worry

The excitement: an agent can do an afternoon of tedious online errands while you do something better. Businesses are betting on it for office work too.

The worry: an agent that can act can also make mistakes at speed. Give one your passwords and it's like handing an eager, brilliant teenager your wallet. Usually fine. Occasionally an expensive lesson.

My take: let the rest of us be the crash-test dummies on agents for a while. The chatbot is plenty to start with.

The scam chapter

This is the chapter I actually worry about. Robots taking over is a movie. Crooks with a new toy is a Tuesday.

Fake voices on the phone

AI can now copy someone's voice from just a few seconds of audio, like a video posted online. Scammers use it for the old "grandparent scam" with a terrifying upgrade. The phone rings. It sounds exactly like me, or Dwyer. There's been an accident, we need money right now, and please don't tell anyone.

The voice will be convincing. That's the point. So don't rely on the voice. Rely on a plan.

What to do (this comes from the FTC)

1. Hang up and call back on the number you already have for that person. If you can't reach them, call someone else in the family. A real emergency survives five minutes of checking.

2. Use a family code word. Many families now pick one secret word that only they know. No code word on an emergency call means hang up. Pick something that has never been posted online: an inside joke works better than a pet's name.

3. Never send money because of a call alone. Gift cards, wire transfers, Bitcoin machines, Zelle and Venmo are the scammer's favorites because the money can't be pulled back.

Fake videos and photos

AI can make a video of a celebrity saying anything. In 2023, Tom Hanks had to warn his fans that a video of "him" selling a dental plan was an AI fake. If a famous person is suddenly selling you a miracle supplement, an investment or a giveaway on Facebook, assume it's fake. The real Tom Hanks is not worried about your gums.

Red flags, AI or not

  • Urgency. "Right now" and "before it's too late" are the scammer's favorite words.
  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone" is never something a real family member in trouble needs.
  • Weird ways to pay. Nobody legitimate asks for gift cards. Not the IRS, not Medicare, not your grandchild.
  • Lookalike apps. In the App Store, check who made it. ChatGPT is made by OpenAI. Claude is made by Anthropic. Anything else with a similar name is a knockoff.

When in doubt: hang up, call me.

Try it tonight

The best way to understand it is to poke at it for ten minutes. You can't break it, and it's free to start.

Getting it on your iPhone: open the App Store and search for ChatGPT (made by OpenAI), Claude (made by Anthropic) or Gemini (made by Google). Check the maker's name before you tap "Get." Then skip the typing: tap the little microphone and talk to it like a person. It handles mumbling better than I do.

Six things to ask it

  1. Mom: "Explain this article like I'm a smart person who hasn't followed the story." (Paste it in, or just say the headline.)
  2. Dad: "I've read every Louis L'Amour and C.J. Box. What should I listen to next, and why?"
  3. Mom: "I loved [your last great romance novel]. Give me five more like it."
  4. Dad: "Help me make a list of what I want to get done this week." Then ask it to read the list back.
  5. Both: "My doctor's report says [the confusing word]. What does it mean, and what should I ask at my next appointment?"
  6. Both: "Make up a bedtime story about a chicken who goes to forest school, for Bonnie, Margaux and Rae." Then tell them a better one yourself. They'll like yours more.

How to read an AI headline

When the headline says...Read it as...
"AI can now do X!"A demo did X once. Wait until regular people use it.
"AGI is two years away"Said by someone raising money. It has been two years away for a while.
"AI will eliminate millions of jobs"Nobody knows. Tasks change faster than jobs do.
"AI passes the bar exam"Great at tests. So was your nephew. Still not your lawyer.
If you remember one thing

AI is a very well-read guesser. Brilliant, fast, and occasionally full of it. Like most of the family at Thanksgiving. Enjoy it, and check its work on anything that matters.
